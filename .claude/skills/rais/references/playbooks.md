# PLAYBOOKS

Standing answers for the decisions that come back every month. Each one has a
**default verdict** — where RAIS starts before hearing the specifics. The default is
not the answer; it is the burden of proof. Facts move it.

---

## 1. "Should I take this job?"

**Default: DO IT, BUT — advance cleared before materials.**

Check, in this order:
1. Realised margin after honest costing + risk loading. Below floor → no.
2. Advance: is there one, and does it clear before he spends?
3. Payment triggers: can TNDK prove them without the client's cooperation?
4. Concentration: does this client go above ~25% of the book?
5. Capacity: is he already personally on three live jobs?
6. Penalty and retention clauses.

**Refuse when:** below floor with no strategic reason · no advance on a job needing
material outlay · client already late on another job · the programme requires him on
site during another project's critical week.

**Take a thin margin only for:** a genuine door-opener with a named follow-on, a
client who pays fast enough that the return on working capital is high even at low
margin, or filling real idle capacity — and say out loud that it is thin, so the
number does not become that client's baseline.

---

## 2. "The client wants a discount."

**Default: DON'T — hold the price, change the scope instead.**

A discount is the most expensive thing he can give: it is 100% margin, it is
permanent for that client, and it travels by word of mouth to their friends.

The arithmetic he should feel: at 25% margin, a 10% discount does not cost 10% — it
costs **40% of the profit**. To earn the same money after it, he has to do roughly
1.7 jobs instead of 1.

**Give instead of money:**
- Remove scope (shorter warranty, no chequered sheet, client-supplied civil work)
- Better terms *for him* — a bigger advance in exchange for a small reduction
- Volume: the price applies at three rooms, not one
- Faster delivery, which costs him planning rather than margin

**Only ever discount against something he receives.** A discount given for free
teaches the client that his first price was not real.

**If he must:** cap it, put it in writing as a one-time project-specific price with
the reason stated, and log it. Never let it become the rate card.

---

## 3. "The client wants longer payment terms / credit."

**Default: DON'T DO IT.**

He is being asked to lend money at 0% to someone with a bigger balance sheet.
Vendors get paid at 30 days; the client is asking for 90. That gap comes out of his
working capital, and working capital is the thing that lets him say no to bad jobs.

**If the relationship is worth it:** price the delay in. Longer terms are a product
with a cost — a 60-day extension on 100,000 is real money out of his pocket, so it
carries a real number in the price. Say that plainly; commercial people understand it.

**Absolute:** never extend terms to a client who is already late on another job. That
is not a payment problem, that is a collection risk compounding.

---

## 4. "Big contract, but the money is blocked." *(the Mesaieed pattern)*

**Default: STOP all committed spend, escalate to a weekly standing item.**

A large contract with an unmet precondition — bank guarantee, performance cheque,
client-side approval — is not an asset. It is an obligation with a story attached.

**Rules while it is blocked:**
1. **Zero vendor commitment** beyond what is covered by cash in hand for that job.
2. A dated action every single week until it clears. Not "following up" — dated.
3. Three weeks with no movement → it becomes the headline of the week and he goes in
   person or in writing to the decision-maker, not the coordinator.
4. Run the honest question out loud: *if this never pays, what have I already spent?*
   That number is the actual exposure.

**Do not let it crowd out mid-size work.** Reserving capacity for a contract that has
not paid is how a company ends up idle and broke at the same time.

## 5. "Should I hire someone?"

**Default: DO IT — for the cheapest hours first, on probation.**

The test is not "can I afford it," it is **"what does the hour I get back earn?"**
If a 3,500/month admin or coordinator removes 40 hours a month of document
production, chasing and driving, and those hours go into pricing and client meetings,
the hire pays for itself several times over — and it is a two-way door.

**Order of hiring for this business:**
1. Admin / coordinator — documents, filing, follow-ups, numbering *(cheapest hours)*
2. Technician — delivers work without him on site
3. Second technician / foreman — turns delivery into a team
4. Estimator or salesman — **last**, and only when there is a proven rate card and
   a written margin floor to hand them

**Never hire before:** the process the hire will run has been written down.
Otherwise he has not delegated, he has just added a person who asks him questions.

**Fund it from:** removed owner-touches, not from an unpaid contract (see the fixed
cost gate in `verdict-engine.md`).

## 6. "Should I buy this vehicle / equipment / tool?"

**Default: NOT YET — rent or subcontract until utilisation proves it.**

Equipment is an asset on paper and a cash consumer in reality. The question is never
"is it useful," it is **"what percentage of the month will it be earning?"**

- Under ~40% utilisation → rent. Every time.
- Over 60% and renting is a recurring cost he can name → buy, from reserves.
- Between → subcontract and revisit in a quarter.

**Buy it outright, not on a facility, unless the facility is cheaper than the cash's
alternative use.** And never buy capacity for a contract that has not paid.

The exception worth taking: a tool that removes a bottleneck he is personally standing
in. That is not equipment, that is buying back time (L8).

---

## 7. "Should I take a bigger workshop / warehouse / office?"

**Default: DON'T DO IT — this is fixed cost dressed as ambition.**

Rent is the hardest cost to reverse: it is a one-way door with a signature, it does
not scale down in a slow quarter, and it usually gets signed in a good month on the
strength of a contract that has not paid.

**Only when:** it is demonstrably costing money now — storage charges, double
handling, work delayed for space — and he can name that number. Then it is not an
expansion, it is a cost saving, and the arithmetic decides it.

**Take the shortest term available**, even at a higher rate. Optionality is worth more
than the discount for signing three years.

---

## 8. "Should I take a partner / investor?"

**Default: DON'T DO IT — this is the most one-way door in business.**

A partner is a marriage with money in it and no easy exit. Almost every reason people
want one is better solved another way:

| Real problem | Better answer than a partner |
|---|---|
| Need cash | Collect faster, take an advance, use a facility |
| Need skills | Hire, or pay a consultant |
| Need the workload shared | Hire — that is an employee, not an owner |
| Need connections / work | Commission on won work, not equity |
| Feel alone in it | An advisor, a mentor, a peer group |

**The only version worth considering:** someone bringing something he genuinely
cannot buy — a licence, a market, a client relationship of real scale — with a written
agreement, a valuation, defined roles, a deadlock mechanism and an exit clause,
reviewed by a lawyer.

**Never give equity for effort or promises.** Equity is forever; effort is monthly.

## 9. "Should I expand into a new line?"

**Default: NOT YET — until the core runs without him.**

Adding a line while he is the bottleneck does not diversify risk, it multiplies the
demand on the single scarce resource. New lines look like growth and behave like a
second job.

**Ranked by distance from what he already does well:**

| Direction | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| AMC / maintenance on his own installed base | **DO IT NOW** | Same clients, same equipment, recurring, high margin, no new competence |
| Spare parts and service calls | DO IT | Fast cash, low commitment, feeds AMC |
| Adjacent refrigeration scope for existing clients | DO IT, BUT | Only with a proven vendor and a costed rate |
| Full kitchen equipment supply / trading | NOT YET | Inventory risk, new vendors, thin margins |
| A different trade entirely | DON'T | Nothing transfers except the company name |

The AMC line is not really an expansion — it is collecting revenue he has already
earned and never invoiced.

## 10. "Should I quote this tender?"

**Default: DO IT, BUT — only if he can name why he would win.**

Tenders cost real hours and most are decided before they are issued. Three questions:
1. **Is there an incumbent?** If yes and there is no complaint about them, he is
   supplying a comparison price for someone else's negotiation.
2. **Is it lowest-price-wins?** Then the winner is whoever made the worst mistake in
   their estimate. Do not compete to be that person.
3. **Can he name a specific advantage** — delivery, reference project, a vendor price
   nobody else has? If he cannot say it in one sentence, he is buying a lottery ticket
   with a day of estimating time.

**Track outcomes.** Win rate is currently unknown (OL-012), which means every tender
decision is being made blind. Ten logged quotes fixes that permanently.

## 11. "The client is not paying."

**Default: DO IT — escalate on a schedule, in writing, and stop work if it continues.**

Politeness is not a collection strategy. Neither is silence. What works is a
predictable, documented, escalating cadence, always in writing, always naming a date
and an amount.

1. **Day 1 overdue** — statement + invoice copy, friendly, states the amount and date
2. **Day 7** — call the person who signs, not the person who processes; confirm in
   writing the same day
3. **Day 14** — written notice to the commercial/project manager, reference the
   contract clause, state a date
4. **Day 21** — formal letter: work stops / material release stops on a named date
5. **Day 30** — stop work. Actually stop. A threat that is not executed teaches the
   client that the next one is not real either.

**Never:** start the next phase to be helpful while the previous one is unpaid. That
converts a collection problem into a bigger collection problem.

**Also check first:** was the invoice actually correct and delivered to the right
person with the right reference? A surprising share of "not paying" is "never
properly invoiced." Hand to `tndk-accounts-team`.

## 12. "Cash is tight this month."

**Default: DO IT — work the collection side before the spending side.**

Order of operations, fastest money first:
1. **Invoice everything invoiceable today.** Any milestone passed without an invoice
   raised is self-inflicted. Check every live job.
2. **Chase the closest, most collectible balance** — not the biggest. Speed beats size
   in a squeeze.
3. **Offer a settlement discount only on genuinely aged money**, and only once.
4. **Delay vendor payments by talking to vendors**, not by going silent. A vendor who
   is told is an ally; a vendor who is surprised is a creditor.
5. **Defer every discretionary purchase** — equipment, vehicles, marketing.
6. **Only then** consider a facility, and only for a timing gap with a named
   collection behind it, never to cover a loss.

**And name the cause**, so the same month does not repeat: was it slow collection,
thin margin, or overcommitted vendor spend? Those are three different diseases.

## 13. "Should I take a loan / bank facility?"

**Default: DO IT, BUT — only against a named, dated receivable.**

Debt is a tool for **timing**, not for **profitability**. It bridges a gap between
work delivered and money that is genuinely coming. It cannot fix a job that was
underpriced — it only postpones the loss and adds interest to it.

- **Acceptable:** invoice discounting or a short facility against a signed contract
  with a milestone already achieved.
- **Acceptable:** a guarantee facility that unlocks an advance — that is the Mesaieed
  shape and it pays for itself immediately.
- **Not acceptable:** funding payroll from a facility with no collection behind it.
- **Never:** personal assets as security for a client's slow payment.

Ask before signing: *if the receivable behind this never arrives, can I still service
it?* If no, the facility is not a bridge, it is a bet.

## 14. "Should I do it myself or subcontract?"

**Default: subcontract the labour, never the client relationship or the pricing.**

Subcontracting converts fixed cost into variable cost and lets him take a job that
would otherwise not fit. It is usually right in a growth phase.

**Keep in-house:** pricing, client contact, final commissioning, and anything the
client would judge him on personally.
**Subcontract:** civil work, transport, overflow installation labour, specialist
electrical.

**Rules:** back-to-back the payment terms so he never pays the subcontractor before
being paid, get the price in writing before committing to the client, and never let
the subcontractor meet the client alone on a job he intends to keep.

---

## 15. "The vendor wants payment in advance."

**Default: DO IT, BUT — only for a vendor with a record, and never above the client
advance already collected.**

The structural rule: **client money in before vendor money out.** If the sequence has
to reverse, that is a job that is financing itself from his reserves, and it must be
a conscious decision with a number attached.

For a new vendor: split it. Part on order, balance on delivery. If they refuse
entirely, that is information about their cash position — and a supplier with a cash
problem is a delivery risk on his project.

---

## 16. "Should I start a second company / brand?"

**Default: DON'T DO IT — unless there is a licence, liability or client reason.**

Two entities means two sets of books, two registrations, double administration, and
confusion in the market — for a business whose named constraint is that everything
routes through one person.

**Legitimate reasons:** a licence one entity cannot hold, ring-fencing genuine
liability, a client or sector that will not deal with the existing entity, or a
partner structure that must be separate.
**Not legitimate:** "it sounds bigger," a different logo, or wanting a fresh start on
a market that a better price list would fix.

He already carries TNDK and DCTS. Two names is enough.

## 17. "I have surplus cash. What do I do with it?"

**Default: reserves first, then time, then annuity, then equipment. In that order.**

1. **Reserve to 3–6 months of fixed cost.** Non-negotiable, and it is not idle money —
   it is what lets him refuse a bad job, which is where margin comes from (L5).
2. **Buy back his time.** The hire or the system that removes the cheapest hours (L8).
3. **Fund the annuity.** Whatever it takes to get the AMC book started — it is the
   highest-multiple asset in this business (L6).
4. **Owner's account.** Take money off the table. A business that has never paid its
   owner is not a success yet.
5. **Equipment**, on the utilisation test (playbook 6).
6. **Only then** growth spending — marketing, a bigger tender bond, new capacity.

**Never:** leave it in the operating account. Money that stays there gets absorbed
into working capital and he will never see it again.

## 18. "I am working fourteen hours a day."

**Default: DO IT — fix it now. This is the ceiling, not a phase.**

This is not a complaint, it is the diagnosis of the whole business (GOALS G6,
`lessons.md` L-004). Every riyal of growth from here has to pass through the same
number of hours, and there are no more hours.

**The exercise, once, honestly:** one week, every task logged with time. Then three
columns — *only Farhan can do this* / *someone could do this with a written process* /
*nobody should be doing this*.

- Column three: **delete it this week.**
- Column two: **write the process, then hire or automate it.**
- Column one: this is the real business. Pricing, client relationships, vendor
  negotiation. Everything else is somebody else's job that he has not given away yet.

**The test of whether it worked:** he can leave Doha for two weeks and quotations
still go out and money still arrives. Until that is true, he owns a job, not a
company — and a job cannot be sold (L11).
