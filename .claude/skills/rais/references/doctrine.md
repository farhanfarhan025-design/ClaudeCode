# DOCTRINE — the twelve laws

The lenses RAIS judges every decision through. Written for a cold-room contracting
business in Qatar, not for a book.

Each law has: the law, why it is true *here*, and what it changes on Monday.

---

## L1 — Cash is the only score

Revenue is an opinion. Profit is an accountant's theory. **Cleared cash is the only
fact.** Everything else is a claim on the future that somebody else has to honour.

**Here.** The book (as at the 13 July 2026 snapshot) is QAR 758,100. Collected:
143,750. Outstanding: 614,350 — and the single largest contract, 400,000, has
collected zero since its LOA was dated 21 May 2026. On paper this is the best year
the company has had. In the bank it is 19% of a good year.

**Monday.** Stop quoting the book. Quote the cash. Every time he says "we've got
758" the correct reply is "we've got 143 and a lot of promises." The weekly number
that matters is *cash collected this week*, not *contracts won this week*.

> A contractor goes bankrupt with a full order book more often than with an empty
> one. The empty one makes you cautious.

---

## L2 — You make your money when you buy, not when you sell

The client sets a ceiling on the price. **The vendor sets the floor on the profit.**
One hour spent on a second and third vendor quote is worth more than a day spent
polishing the client proposal, because it moves the number that survives to the end.

**Here.** The rate card in the pricing guide has **no recorded verification date**
(`open_loops.md` OL-009). Every price quoted off it is built on costs of unknown age.
On a 100,000 job, a 6% drift in panel and unit prices is 6,000 — gone, silently,
before the quotation was even sent.

**Monday.** No contract above ~100,000 gets priced off memory. Three vendor quotes,
dated, on file. Ask for the price *twice* — the second ask is free and usually works.

---

## L3 — Never finance your customer

A main contractor with a treasury department is not short of money. When he asks for
90-day terms, he is not asking for a favour — he is asking to use your capital
instead of his, at 0%, while you pay your vendors at 30.

**Here.** The pattern to watch is Mesaieed: 400,000 committed, advance blocked behind
a bank guarantee and a performance cheque, ten weeks and nothing collected. That is
not a client problem, it is a *structure* problem — the trigger for the money is
something the client controls and TNDK does not.

**Monday.** Three non-negotiables on any contract above 50,000:
1. An advance that clears **before** materials are ordered.
2. Payment triggers TNDK can prove unilaterally — delivery note signed, not
   "after client's internal certification."
3. Retention capped, with a stated release date, in writing.

If a client will not give an advance, he is telling you something about his own cash.
Believe him.

---

## L4 — Price the risk, not the cost

Cost-plus-a-habit is how a written 30% policy turns into a delivered 14.6%. The
markup should move with what can go wrong: a new client, a tight programme, a
penalty clause, a difficult site, an unknown vendor — every one of those is a
probability of losing money, and it gets **paid for in the price** or it gets paid
for out of the owner's pocket.

**Here.** `lessons.md` L-001 is the whole lesson: *a rule that is not computed at the
decision point is a preference, not a control.* The pricing guide's own worked
example — in the same document that names 30% — prices a job at 14.6%.

**Monday.** Every quote carries a computed realised margin before it is sent. Risk
loading is explicit, not vibes: new client +5, penalty clause +5, programme under
4 weeks +5, unverified vendor rate +5. Then price. See `verdict-engine.md` for the
thresholds.

---

## L5 — The deals you refuse make you rich

Capacity is finite and there is exactly one of him. A job taken at 12% does not just
earn 12% — it **occupies the slot** a 28% job would have used, and it teaches that
client, permanently, what your price is. The cost of a bad job is the good job you
could not take, plus every future job that client will now expect at that number.

**Here.** Win rate is unknown — only won jobs are recorded (`open_loops.md` OL-012).
So nobody knows whether the low pricing is even buying the work. He may be
discounting against competitors who were never going to win anyway.

**Monday.** Log every quotation and its outcome. Within ten quotes he will know his
real win rate, and the discount conversation stops being a guess. And the first time
he walks away from a low-margin job and the client comes back at his number, he will
never price the same way again.

---

## L6 — Sell the machine, keep the meter

The install is a transaction. The **installed base** is an asset. A cold room sold is
paid once. A cold room maintained is paid every year, at higher margin, with no
tender, no competitor, and a phone call instead of a bid.

This is the single biggest structural difference between a busy contractor and a
wealthy one. Transaction revenue resets to zero every January. Annuity revenue
starts the year already banked, and — this is the part that matters — **a buyer pays
a far higher multiple for a maintenance book than for a project pipeline**, because
one is predictable and the other is hope.

**Here.** AMC contracted value is QAR **0**. AMC appears exactly once in the entire
system — a clause on one LOA. Every room ever installed is an annuity that was never
collected.

**Monday.** Two lists. (1) Every room installed, with a warranty end date. (2) Every
one of those clients, with an AMC price. The proposal goes out 60 days before the
warranty expires, while he is still the man who knows the equipment.

---

## L7 — Concentration kills faster than competition

Every big contract feels like the best news of the year. Two of them feel like
having arrived. What it actually is: **one client's decision can now end the
company.**

**Here.** Top two clients = **86.2%** of the book. The larger — 52.8% — has paid
nothing. If Mesaieed goes quiet, that is not a bad quarter, that is a solvency event,
because vendor commitments were made against it.

**Monday.** Two numbers on the weekly page: top-2 concentration %, and committed
vendor spend exposed to uncollected contracts. Direction on the first: **down, by
adding mid-size work — never by losing the big ones.** The fix for concentration is
more revenue, not less.

---

## L8 — Buy back your time in order of its hourly value

Rank every hour he spends by what it earns. Pricing a 250,000 contract: thousands of
riyals an hour. Negotiating with a vendor: hundreds. Chasing a receipt, formatting an
invoice, driving a part across Doha: tens, or less. **Delegate strictly from the
bottom.** The first hire is never a partner or a salesman — it is the person who
takes the 30-riyal hours off the man who has 3,000-riyal hours available.

**Here.** `lessons.md` L-004 named it exactly: the existing skills made the
bottleneck *faster*, not *removable*. Sales, quoting, procurement, invoicing,
collections and filing all still start and end with him.

**Monday.** Track owner-touches per completed job (GOALS G6). One week of honest
logging tells him what to give away first. The rule for what he keeps: **pricing,
client relationships, vendor negotiation.** Everything else is a candidate.

---

## L9 — Never bet the company on one job

There is a number above which a single job's failure — non-payment, a penalty, a
scope fight — takes the whole business with it. Most owners have never calculated it
and discover it once.

**Monday.** Two caps, written down:
- **Committed vendor spend against any single uncollected contract** must not exceed
  the cash actually in hand for that contract, plus a stated tolerance he sets
  deliberately.
- **No single contract's unsecured exposure** should exceed what the company could
  absorb as a total loss and still pay salaries for three months.

If a job breaches either, the answer is not "be careful." It is a bank guarantee, a
bigger advance, a milestone restructure, or no.

---

## L10 — Pay yourself first; owner's pay is a cost, not a leftover

If the owner's income is whatever survives the month, the business is being run for
everyone except the owner. The vendor is paid, the labour is paid, the landlord is
paid, and the man carrying every risk in the company takes the remainder.

**Monday.** A fixed monthly owner's drawing, set deliberately and paid on a date, as
a cost line in the job costing. Separate accounts, mechanically:

| Account | Purpose | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| **Operating** | day-to-day in and out | receipts land here |
| **Reserve** | survival | target 3–6 months of fixed cost; do not touch for opportunities |
| **Tax / obligation** | retentions, guarantees, statutory | funded on receipt, never borrowed from |
| **Owner** | his money | transferred on a date, not on a feeling |

Money that never leaves the operating account is never actually his — it is working
capital he happens to be standing next to.

---

## L11 — Assets over income

Income buys a lifestyle and stops the day he does. Assets pay when he is not
in the room. Ranked by what they are worth to a contractor, best first:

1. **A maintenance book** (recurring, high margin, sells at a multiple)
2. **A team that delivers without him** (turns his time into capacity, and turns the
   company into something sellable — a business that requires the owner *is a job*)
3. **A repeat-client base with pricing power** (won on reliability, not on price)
4. **Cash reserves** (the only thing that lets him say no, which is where the margin
   comes from — see L5)
5. **Equipment and vehicles** — assets on paper, but they consume cash, depreciate,
   and only earn when utilised. Buy the fifth one last.

**The arithmetic he should have in his head.** A contractor earning 300k a year of
owner's income has an income. A contractor with a 300k-a-year maintenance book and a
team that runs it has **something a buyer will pay a multiple for** — plus the income.
Same yearly number, entirely different net worth.

---

## L12 — Decide fast on reversible things

Most decisions are **two-way doors**: try it, and if it fails, walk back out. A
vendor trial, a price test, a new service line on one job, a hire on probation.
These should be decided in minutes, because the cost of being wrong is one job and
the cost of deliberating is weeks of not knowing.

A few are **one-way doors**: a partner, a personal guarantee, a long lease, a
contract signed with a penalty clause, a public price cut to a major client. These
get every hour they need and a night's sleep.

**The failure mode of a busy owner is inverting these** — agonising over a 3,000-riyal
tool purchase while signing a 400,000 contract with a payment trigger he does not
control, on the same afternoon.

**Monday.** Before deliberating on anything, ask one question: *can I undo this?*
If yes, decide now.

## The mindset, in one table

| Busy operator | Owner-capitalist |
|---|---|
| Chases revenue | Chases collected margin |
| Wins the job | Chooses the job |
| Discounts to survive the month | Walks away and keeps the price |
| Is proud of being needed everywhere | Is proud of what runs without him |
| Buys equipment when cash arrives | Buys reserves first, capacity second |
| Measures the order book | Measures cash, margin, concentration, owner-touches |
| Sells installations | Sells installations and keeps the meter running |
| Reacts to the loudest client | Runs a weekly cycle and decides in advance |
| One good year at a time | A book of recurring revenue that compounds |
| Owns a job | Owns an asset |

## The uncomfortable part

The market is not what is keeping him from being rich. Qatar is not short of cold
room work — he has 758,100 of it on the books. What is keeping him from being rich,
on the evidence in his own files:

1. He prices below his own written policy and did not know it. *(14.6% against 30%)*
2. He does not collect fast enough. *(81% of the book uncollected)*
3. He sells installations and gives away the annuity. *(AMC: zero)*
4. He is personally on the critical path of every single job. *(G6)*
5. He is concentrated in two clients and one of them has not paid. *(86.2%)*

Every one of those is fixable and none of them require a bigger market, a partner,
or more capital. Fixing the first alone — 14.6% to 25% on the current book — is
roughly **QAR 79,000**. That is the whole argument for taking this seriously.
